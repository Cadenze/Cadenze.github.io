For the first time, legendary violinist Itzhak Perlman visits Kitchener, Ontario.
I am lucky enough to be living in Kitchener at the same time,
and the hall that he was playing in, Centre in the Square, is merely a 10-minute walk from my apartment.
Even though this recital lands on a weekday evening,
these circumstances basically guarantee my attendance.

## Programme

Itzhak Perlman (violin), Rohan de Silva (piano)

19th November, 2026

- **Mozart**, Sonata for Violin and Piano No. 32, K. 454
- **Strauss**, Sonata for Violin and Piano, Op. 18
- **Dvorak**, Sonatina, Op. 100, B. 183
- Works announced from the stage

Centre in the Square, Kitchener, Ontario

## First half

It is worth noting that the concert hall is what I would consider in the style of an opera hall;
however, perhaps with newer material, the reverb was not as pronounced as I originally thought.
Despite that, the piano was held at half stick throughout the night,
which we will later see to be a justified choice.

### Mozart

Perlman opens with the Mozart sonata,
which as with all other Mozart works, or perhaps even all other classical works,
requires the utmost care while sounding effortless.
As I have listened to recordings of Perlman since I was a child,
I had great expectations, and unfortunately those expectations were not met.

The *Largo -- Allegro* opens with unusual shakiness,
and the violin sounds almost uncomfortable playing in such a hall.
Despite good, principled chamber music playing throughout the *Andante*,
these two movements were marred by audible mistakes regarding intonation.
The *Allegretto* at the end provides us brief respite,
with a noticeable difference in the quality of play.

### Strauss

While the Mozart was expected to be light,
the Strauss is surely heavier and requires a lot more emotion from the musicians.
The uncertainty seemed to have carried over from the Mozart,
and repeated technical mistakes during the *Allegro ma non troppo* distracted us
from an otherwise unremarkable interpretation of the sonata.
The performance seems to improve from this point onwards,
with the *Improvisation* sounding a bit more uptight as opposed to improvisational,
and the *Finale* struggling only from issues regarding balance.

## Second half

After a lengthy intermission of almost 20 minutes,
Perlman returns to the stage in what I think is much better shape.

### Dvorak

In contrast to the first half,
the violin has suddenly gained a life of its own,
and projected its sound a lot better.
There is a clear change in Perlman's demeanor, and he appears to be a bit more relaxed.
This comfort has brought a sense of lightness that was absent in the Mozart.

### Pieces announced from the stage

In true Perlman fashion, he excels when playing the small pieces that are crowd-pleasers.
He starts with **Kreisler**, Andantino in the style of Martini.
A light joke about Martini ironically dying from liver failure seems to lighten the mood of the entire night.

This is then followed up with another transcription of Kreisler,
**Tchaikovsky**'s Chant sans parole (song without words).
Once again, he jokes about the word "parole",
and mentioned that "sans parole" as a pun lands better in Canada than in the US.

A third piece by **Kreisler**, the well-known Liebesfreud (love-joy),
receives tremendous applause compared to the rest of the programme.
Unexpectedly coming from an 80 year old man, upon hearing this applause,
he says that "we're cooking here".

The recital is then brought to a close with the theme from *Schindler's List* by **John Williams**.
A standing ovation follows, and he is then brought back out for an encore,
where he delivers a Humoresque by **Tchaikovsky**;
upon hearing some oohs and aahs from the audience,
he mentions that it is not the famous one written by Dvorak.

Overall, the recital is a story of two halves,
and although it is a shame that the Strauss sonata, which I have looked forward to for weeks, did not deliver,
it is great to see that Perlman has not lost his sharp wit and endearing charm.
